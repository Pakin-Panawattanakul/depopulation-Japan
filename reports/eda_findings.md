# Step 6: exploratory findings

Generated by `python src/eda.py`. The figures are in `reports/figures/`, and the numbers are in `reports/eda_summary.json` and `reports/prefecture_peaks.csv`.

## 1. National picture (figs 01, 02)
- Japan's population **peaked in 2008 at 128.08 million**.
- **Deaths have exceeded births every year since 2005**, and the gap keeps widening.
- Since then, national decline is almost entirely *natural*. Averaged over prefectures, 2015–2020 natural change was −2.33% and total change −2.30%. Migration mostly moves people *between* regions, and nets to roughly zero for the country.

## 2. When decline started, and historical events (figs 03, 05)
Year of each prefecture's peak population in the 1975–2024 data:

| Peak period | Prefectures |
|---|---|
| 1976–1985 (before the bubble) | 11: Akita, Wakayama, Aomori, Nagasaki, Shimane, Kochi, Yamaguchi, Iwate, Ehime, Oita, Kagoshima |
| Bubble era 1986–1991 | 3: Tokushima, Yamagata, Tottori |
| After the bubble, 1992–1999 | 10, incl. Hokkaido, Niigata, Hiroshima, Fukushima |
| 2000–2004 | 10, incl. Ishikawa, Nagano, Miyagi, Kyoto |
| 2005–2010 (national peak) | 5: Tochigi, Shizuoka, Mie, Hyogo, Osaka |
| 2011–2019 | 2: Aichi, Shiga |
| 2020 or later / still growing | 6: Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, Chiba, Fukuoka, Okinawa |

- Decline spread **from the periphery inward**: first northern Tohoku, Shikoku, Kyushu and San'in, then the regional hubs, then the industrial belt, and last the big metro areas.
- **The first wave was driven by migration.** In 1985–1990 every prefecture still had more births than deaths, yet 18 were shrinking because of out-migration during the bubble.
- Prefectures shrinking per 5-year period: 1 (1980–85) → 18 (1985–90) → 23 (1995–2000) → 32 (2000–05) → **38 (2005–10 onward)**.
- Limitation: data starts in 1975, so declines before then (e.g. Shimane and Kagoshima in the 1950s–60s) aren't visible.

## 3. Migration toward Tokyo follows the economy (fig 04)
Net inflow into the Tokyo area (Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, Chiba) per 1,000 residents:

| Year | Net inflow | Context |
|---|---|---|
| 1987 | +5.3 | bubble peak |
| **1994** | **−0.5** | net *outflow*, after the bubble burst |
| 2007 | +4.4 | |
| ~2011 | +1.8 | after the Lehman shock |
| 2021 | +2.2 | COVID |
| 2024 | +3.2 | |

- The rest of Japan mirrors this pattern. It was the only time the rest of Japan gained people on net (1993–96), when the Tokyo economy was weak.
- **Takeaway for interpretation:** social change responds to the business cycle, and booms in Tokyo drain the regions.
- Tokyo proper had *negative* net migration in the 1980s and early 1990s, as people moved out to the suburbs in Saitama, Chiba and Kanagawa. Moves back to central Tokyo began in the late 1990s (fig 06).

## 4. Municipalities (figs 07, 08)
In 2015–2020:
- **82%** of municipalities shrank.
- **92%** had more deaths than births.
- **14%** lost more than 10% of their population in five years, up from 2% in 1995–2000.

Size is the strongest pattern. Median 5-year change, 2015–2020:

| Population in 2015 | Total | Natural | Social |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 5k | −9.4% | −6.4 | −2.9 |
| 200k+ | +0.2% | −1.1 | +1.0 |

Small municipalities lose people both through deaths and through out-migration.

## 5. First look at factors (fig 09; prefecture panel, rank correlation)

| | Strongest correlates |
|---|---|
| **Natural change** | aging rate (−0.97), share of women aged 20–39 (+0.92), unmarried rate of women 30–34 (−0.80), vacancy rate (−0.80), doctors per capita (−0.76; areas with many elderly have many doctors) |
| **Social change** | fiscal strength (+0.60), relative income (+0.53), distance to the nearest metro (−0.48), land price (+0.44), population size (+0.41), university outflow share (−0.41), job-openings ratio (+0.35) |

- **The split into natural and social change is confirmed**: natural change is about *age structure*, social change is about *economy and geography*. The two need separate models.
- ⚠ These are pooled correlations across all periods. Aging rises over time while natural change falls, so part of the −0.97 is a shared trend. Step 7/8 handle this with period fixed effects and within-period comparisons.
