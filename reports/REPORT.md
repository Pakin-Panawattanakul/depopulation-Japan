# Factors of regional population decline in Japan

JAIST K417EJ capstone. Data: e-Stat, Social and Demographic Statistics System (SSDS, 社会・人口統計体系), 47 prefectures 1975–2024 and 1,741 municipalities 1980–2024 (merger-adjusted). Every number below comes from `reports/*.md|csv|json`, generated by the scripts in `src/`.

## 1. Question and approach

1. **When** did regional decline start, and does it line up with historical events?
2. **What contributes most** to depopulation?
3. **Do the main factors change** over time (≈30, 20 and 10 years ago vs. now)?
4. Can we **predict** regional population 5 and 10 years ahead?

Main design choices (details in `docs/datasets.md`):
- **Split population change** into *natural change* (births − deaths) and *social change* (net migration), because they have different causes.
- **Unit = 5-year period** between censuses: 374 prefecture and 8,585 municipal observations.
- **Features are measured at the start of each period**, to avoid reverse causality and to make forecasting honest.
- **Excluded:** Fukushima after 2010 (nuclear evacuation, decision D1) and Miyake village 1995–2005 (volcanic evacuation, D6).

## 2. When decline started (step 6, `reports/eda_findings.md`)

- **National:** Japan's population peaked in **2008 at 128.08M**. Deaths have exceeded births every year **since 2005**.
- **Decline moved from the periphery inward.** By year of peak population:
  - 1976–85: 11 prefectures (Akita, Aomori, Iwate, Shimane, Kochi, Nagasaki, Kagoshima and others);
  - 1986–91 (bubble era): 3;
  - 1992–99: 10;
  - 2000–04: 10;
  - 2005–10: 5;
  - 2011–19: 2;
  - still growing in 2020 or later: 6 (Tokyo, Kanagawa, Saitama, Chiba, Fukuoka, Okinawa).
- **The first wave was migration-driven.** In 1985–90 every prefecture still had more births than deaths, yet 18 shrank because people left during the **bubble economy**.
- **Migration to Tokyo follows the business cycle.** Net inflow into the Tokyo area (per 1,000 residents):

  | Year | Net inflow | Event |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1987 | +5.3 | bubble peak |
  | 1994 | −0.5 | after the bubble burst |
  | 2007 | +4.4 | |
  | ~2011 | +1.8 | after the Lehman shock |
  | 2021 | +2.2 | COVID |
  | 2024 | +3.2 | |

  The rest of Japan mirrors this.
- **Municipalities, 2015–20:** 82% shrank, 92% had more deaths than births, and 14% lost more than 10% in five years (up from 2% in 1995–2000). Municipalities under 5,000 people had a median change of −9.4% over 5 years.

## 3. What contributes most (steps 7–8, `reports/feature_selection.md` and `feature_selection_interpretation.md`, `reports/models_explanatory.md`)

**Natural change is almost fully explained by age structure.**
- Prefecture model, R² 0.98:
  - aging rate: **−1.92 pp per SD** of 5-year change;
  - total fertility rate: +0.66;
  - other factors: small.
- Municipal model, aging rate: −2.70 pp per SD (within-prefecture R² 0.86).
- Women aged 20–39 carry the same information as aging rate (r > 0.8).

**Social change (migration) follows economic strength and the job market.**

| Comparison | Factors | Coefficients |
|---|---|---|
| Between prefectures (period FE) | aging rate | **−1.21** |
| | income | +0.36 |
| | unemployment | −0.36 |
| | job-openings ratio | +0.25 |
| | snow days | −0.15 |
| Within a prefecture over time (two-way FE) | **unemployment** | **−1.05** |
| | job-openings ratio | +0.33 |
| Municipalities (within prefecture) | aging rate | −0.93 |
| | fiscal strength | +0.58 |
| | income | +0.53 |

- Within a prefecture over time: when a prefecture's own job market weakens, more people leave.
- Social change is much harder to explain than natural change: CV R² about 0.4–0.6 for prefectures and 0.2–0.35 for municipalities.
- ⚠ Fiscal strength, income, land price and urban share overlap strongly (VIF ≈ 10–15). With 47 prefectures they act as one *economic-core* factor.

**ANOVA (share of variance, η²):**
- Prefecture natural change is mostly *when* (period 68%).
- Prefecture social change is mostly *where* (region 32%, period 5%).
- For municipalities, **size class** explains more than region (17% vs 4% of total change).
- Tukey HSD on municipal social change, 2015–20: Kanto (+0.85) is significantly above all other regions, Hokkaido (−3.16) significantly below.

## 4. Do the factors change over time? (step 9, `reports/time_varying.md`)

Yes. An interaction F-test rejects constant coefficients at both levels and for both targets (all p < 0.0001).

| Era | Prefecture migration | Municipal migration |
|---|---|---|
| ~30 years ago (1985–90) | **unemployment** (−1.60 per SD), aging, **snow**: hard times and harsh climate pushed people out | (no municipal data) |
| ~20 years ago (1995–2000) | **income** takes over (52% of explained variance); explanatory power at its lowest (R² 0.32), after the bubble burst | **income** dominant; **urban share −1.24** (people leaving dense areas for the suburbs) |
| ~10 years ago (2005–10) | income (37%) and aging | **fiscal strength** dominant |
| Now (2015–20) | **income** (39%) and **aging** (37%); unemployment's share is ~1% | **fiscal strength** (37%); urban share **+0.23** (return to cities) |

- **Natural change:** aging rate's share stays at 54–69%. Fertility's share falls from 24% to 8%, while income's share rises from 15% to 23%.
- **Interpretation:** migration used to be pushed by unemployment and climate. Now it is pulled by income and the local tax base, and decline in aging areas reinforces itself.

## 5. Forecasts (step 8b, `reports/forecast.md`)

**Out-of-time backtests,** mean absolute error of 5-year change:

| Model | Prefectures | Municipalities |
|---|---|---|
| Naive persistence | 0.73 pp | 2.09 |
| Best model | **0.58 pp** (ridge, natural + social parts) | **1.82** (gradient boosting, parts) |

- **10-year horizon (2010→20, prefectures):** 1.22 pp vs 1.88 pp for naive.
- **Forecast from 2020, 46 prefectures** (excluding Fukushima): **124.31M → 122.72M (2025) → 118.99M (2030)**.
  - Akita −14.6% by 2030; Iwate and Aomori −12.6%.
  - Tokyo +3.0%; Okinawa +1.3%.
- **Municipalities:** 86% predicted to shrink 2020–25, and 64% to lose more than 10% by 2030.
- **Live check against the official 2024 estimates:**
  - correlation **0.97**, MAE 0.80 pp;
  - but the model is **systematically ~0.8 pp too optimistic**. Decline sped up after 2020 (birth slump, more deaths, a COVID outflow from Tokyo), which no training period contained.
  - Okinawa started shrinking (−0.1% vs. +1.1% predicted), and Tokyo grew less than predicted (+0.9% vs. +2.0%).

## 5b. Why are fewer babies born? (step 11, `reports/fertility_findings.md`)

**Fewer marriages, not smaller families:**
- The married share of women 25–39 fell from **84.5% (1980) to 54.0% (2020)**.
- Births per married woman *rose* 9% over the same period.
- Age-by-age check, 1980→2005: fewer married women would have cut the fertility rate by 0.80; more births per married woman added back 0.31.

**Marriage is lower where** (robust in both comparisons, between prefectures and within a prefecture over time):
- unemployment is high (−0.89 / −0.73 pp per SD);
- homes are small (floor area +1.19 / +1.76).

**Marriage also rises when a prefecture's own income and men's wages rise** (+0.88 and +1.26, within a prefecture).

**Cities have lower fertility,** both overall and per married couple (urban share −0.06 / −0.10 TFR per SD).

**Women's employment** shows no consistent effect.

**Childcare ratios** (nurseries per child, waiting lists) are driven by the number of children themselves (reverse causation), so they can't be read as causes.

## 6. Limitations

- **Short series:** data starts in 1975, so declines before then (1950s–60s rural exodus) are not visible.
- **Overlapping variables:** with 47 prefectures, overlapping economic variables can't be separated (they act as one "economic-core" factor). Coefficients describe associations, not proven causes.
- **Coarse distance:** distance to metro is measured from prefectural capitals, so it says nothing *within* a prefecture.
- **Missing municipal data:** municipal migration data exists only from 2018 (we use the residual instead), and there is no municipal "women 20–39" band.
- **Optimistic forecasts:** they come from 2020 values and miss the post-2020 acceleration. The 80% intervals are based on only 1–3 backtests.
- **No official baseline:** a comparison with the official IPSS projections was planned but not done (their data isn't on e-Stat).
