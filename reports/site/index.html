Japan's Shrinking Regions地域の人口減少

When regional decline began, what drives it, whether the drivers have changed, and how far it will go by 2030. Population change is split throughout into natural change (births − deaths) and social change (net migration), because each has its own causes.

Q1 · When did decline start?

From the periphery inward, in waves set by the economy

The first prefectures peaked around 1980, well before the national peak in 2008. The bubble years pulled young people to Tokyo, and each later wave of decline reached closer to the big cities. Six prefectures were still growing in 2020.

The Tokyo area's net inflow rose to +5.3 per 1,000 at the bubble peak in 1987 and turned negative in 1994 after the crash. The rest of Japan gained people on net only in those few years. Inflow recovered to +4.4 by 2007, dropped after the Lehman shock, dipped again with COVID in 2021, and was back to +3.2 by 2024.

Q2 · What contributes most?

Age structure sets the death toll; jobs and income steer migration

Natural change is almost fully predictable from today's age structure. Migration, the part policy can influence, follows local income, the job market and the tax base, and is much harder to explain.

  • Municipalities: aging (−0.93), fiscal strength (+0.58) and income (+0.53) lead.
  • Income, fiscal strength, land price and urban share overlap strongly and act as one "economic core". With 47 prefectures they can't be separated.
Q3 · Have the main factors changed?

Yes: from push by unemployment to pull by income

An F-test rejects "same effects in every period" at both levels (p < 0.0001). Thirty years ago unemployment and harsh winters pushed people out. Today income and the local tax base pull them in, and aging areas keep losing.

~30 yrs ago · 1985–90

Unemployment (−1.60 per SD), aging and snow explain migration between prefectures.

~20 yrs ago · 1995–2000

Income takes over (52% of explained variance). Explanatory power is at its lowest just after the bubble. Municipalities: people leave dense areas for the suburbs (urban share −1.24).

~10 yrs ago · 2005–10

Income (37%) and aging. Fiscal strength becomes the main municipal factor.

Now · 2015–20

Income (39%) and aging (37%). Unemployment ~1%. Urban share turns positive (+0.23): people are moving back into cities.

Q4 · How far will it go?

About 5 million fewer people by 2030, mostly outside the big cities

Show the forecast as a table

How much to trust it

  • Out-of-time backtests on 2005, 2010 and 2015: mean absolute error pp of 5-year change, vs pp for "the last five years repeat".
  • 10-year horizon (2010→20): 1.22 pp vs 1.88 pp.
  • Live check against the official 2024 estimates: correlation 0.97, but about 0.8 pp too optimistic. Decline sped up after 2020 (birth slump, more deaths, a COVID outflow from Tokyo), and no training period contained that.
  • Okinawa began to shrink (−0.1% vs +1.1% predicted), and Tokyo grew less than predicted (+0.9% vs +2.0%).

Limitations

  • The series starts in 1975, so the 1950s–60s rural exodus isn't visible.
  • Coefficients are associations, not proven causes. Overlapping economic variables can't be separated with 47 prefectures.
  • Distance to metros is measured from prefectural capitals. Municipal migration is the residual of population change minus natural change, because municipal move counts start only in 2018.
  • Forecasts use 2020 values and miss the post-2020 acceleration. The 80% intervals rest on one to three backtests. A comparison with the official IPSS projections is still to do.
